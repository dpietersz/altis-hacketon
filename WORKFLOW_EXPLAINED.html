Altis hackathon — data pipeline walkthrough

How the cash flow pipeline works

A plain-English walkthrough of the data work behind the CFO dashboard — Tier 1 (cash forecast) plus Tier 2 (weather-adjusted). Read top to bottom — no finance or engineering background needed.

The big picture in three sentences

Altis owns four roofing companies in the Netherlands. The CFO needs to know how much cash will land in the bank over the next 13 weeks, whether any company is at risk of breaking a loan rule, and how those numbers change if the weather goes bad. We built a small data pipeline in Databricks that turns ~25 messy Excel files into clean, dashboard-ready data — that is what this document explains.

The journey, in one picture

The data goes through four stages. Each stage takes the previous one as input and produces something more useful.

Below: what happens at each step, in plain English.

Step 1 of 5Clean up the messy raw files (Silver layer)

The client gave us about 25 Excel files. They come from two different accounting systems (Yuki and Exact) plus a couple of summary files. Each system organises its files differently:

  • Yuki files start with a 12-row "cover page" before the real data begins
  • Exact files start cleanly with the header on row 1
  • Some files overlap — the client sent us both a "full year" file and a "first 5 months" file for the same account

We read every file, force them all into one common shape, and remove duplicate rows. The result is a single tidy table called silver/bookings.parquet.

Number out: 36,565 raw bookings → 31,734 unique bookings after duplicates removed (about 4,831 overlaps cleanly dropped).

Step 2 of 5Add business labels (Gold layer — Classify)

The cleaned table is just rows of numbers. To chart anything useful, every booking needs two labels.

Label 2: what kind of cash flow is it?

Every booking gets sorted into one of four "drivers" — types of cash movement:

  • milestone_in money coming in from a customer paying an invoice
  • materials_out money going out to buy roofing materials
  • subcon_out money going out to subcontractors
  • labour_out money going out for wages
Important: the client only sent us sales-side data. So every real booking we have right now is milestone_in. The three "money out" lines are estimated later, in Step 3, from a margin assumption.

We also pull a monthly revenue summary from a JSON file the client gave us. This file is the only source that includes Andijk — so the dashboard can still show all four companies side by side, even though we only have detailed bookings for three.

Step 3 of 5Predict the next 13 weeks (Gold layer — Forecast)

This is the heart of the dashboard. For each company, for each of the next 13 weeks, for each of three scenarios, we predict money in and money out.

Three plain-English rules

Money in (next 13 weeks). The CFO told us customers pay invoices about 30 days after receiving them. So next week's cash receipts equal the invoices we sent ~30 days ago. For weeks beyond what we have data for, we predict invoicing from the recent weekly average, adjusted for the time of year (roofing has a strong summer peak, quiet winter).

Money out. We don't have supplier data yet, so we estimate it. A roofing company typically keeps about 22% of revenue as gross profit, so ~78% goes out as costs. We split those costs across materials, subcontractors and wages using a default mix — when real supplier data arrives, we just update the numbers.

Three scenarios. The wet/dry scenarios are the weather toggle the challenge asks for.

Base

Business as usual.

Wet quarter

Rain delays work. Revenue −10% and payments take 7 extra days for the first 6 weeks.

Dry quarter

Great weather. Revenue +5% and payments arrive 3 days faster for the first 6 weeks.

No AI, no machine learning. The CFO would never sign off on a number that came from a black box. Every forecast figure traces back to specific bookings plus named assumptions, so a controller can defend any number on the dashboard.

How "confident" each forecast number is

Each week's prediction is tagged with how much real data went into it:

  • Observed — the receipts come from invoices already sent. Real numbers.
  • Mixed — partly real, partly predicted.
  • Projected — fully predicted from recent rate + seasonality.
  • Derived — always used for the "money out" lines (estimated, not observed).

The dashboard uses these tags to colour-code certainty (solid lines vs dashed).

Step 4 of 5Check the safety limits (Gold layer — Covenant)

When a private-equity firm or a bank lends money to a company, they put rules in the loan contract — for example, "you must keep at least €X in the bank at all times". These rules are called covenants. Breaking one can trigger penalties or even let the lender call the loan back, so the CFO watches the cash buffer constantly.

"Headroom" is just the gap between current cash and the minimum the contract requires. Lots of headroom = relaxed. Headroom shrinking = time to act.

Step 5 of 6 — Tier 2Layer the weather into the forecast

Tier 1's wet/dry scenarios were arbitrary toggles ("let's pretend revenue drops 10% in the wet case"). Tier 2 replaces those toggles with actual weather data from KNMI — three daily-weather files for the cities where the companies operate.

Why weather matters for a roofing company

Roofers can't work safely in heavy rain, freezing temperatures, or strong wind. So the weather directly determines whether the day produces invoiceable work or not. The chain is:

Weather → lost work days → fewer invoices sent → less cash arrives 30 days later

We need to turn "it rained for 3 days" into "expect €X less cash next week". That's what this step does.

Rule 1 — score each historical day

For every day in 2020–2026, for each company, we look at the rainfall, temperature and wind, and assign a "work-day score":

Daily roofing-productivity rules

ConditionScorePlain meaning
Rainfall > 10 mm, or mean temp < 0 °C, or wind > 10 m/s0.0Day lost — no roofing work possible
Rainfall 1–10 mm (and no other blockers)0.5Half day — some crews work, some don't
Otherwise (dry, mild, calm)1.0Full work day

Thresholds are roofing-industry rules of thumb. The controller can edit them in notebook 11.

Rule 2 — roll up to weekly

For each Monday-week we add up the day-scores across Mon–Fri and divide by 5:

work_day_ratio = sum(day_scores Mon-Fri) / 5

So 1.0 = a perfect work week, 0.6 = 40% of working time lost to weather, 0.0 = nothing got done that week.

Rule 3 — what does "wet" and "dry" actually look like?

Instead of arbitrary toggles, we now derive scenarios from historical patterns. For each company we look at how each month of the year actually played out across 2022–2024:

Scenario weather (from historical climate normals)

ScenarioHow we compute itPlain meaning
BaseMedian work_day_ratio for that month over 3 yearsA typical month
Wet10th percentile (one of the wettest in ten)A bad month — what a real wet quarter looked like
Dry90th percentile (one of the best in ten)A great month — what a real dry quarter looked like

This is honest: we're saying "this is what a real wet quarter looked like for this company" rather than "let's guess 10% worse".

Rule 4 — adjust the forecast

For each future week, for each company, for each scenario, we look up that month's work_day_ratio and apply two corrections:

Weather adjustment applied to every forecast row

Expected revenuebaseline_revenue × work_day_ratio
Implied DSO delay (tooltip)(1 − work_day_ratio) × 14 days
Expected outflowsRe-derived from the new revenue (same 22% margin + 45/35/20 split)

So if the model says "60% productive in May for Heeze", then May's revenue forecast drops by 40% and we tell the dashboard "expect ~6 extra days of cash delay".

The honest correction. Every Tier 2 scenario lands 10–25% lower than the matching Tier 1 scenario. This isn't bad news — it's the model finally acknowledging that roofers don't get a full work-week every week. Tier 1 implicitly assumed they did.

What we don't have yet (so we can't fake)

  • A real future weather forecast. KNMI only sent us historical data. For now the next 13 weeks use "what the climate normally does in this month". When we wire a forecast API later, the model doesn't change — just the lookup.
  • Weather for Andijk (no KNMI file given). We use the average of the other three companies as a stand-in.

If something changes

The pipeline is built so that the team can update things without touching code:

  • The client sends new Excel files → drop them in the raw/ folder, rerun notebook 03 onward.
  • The CFO gives us a new DSO or margin number → edit the ASSUMPTIONS table in notebook 06.
  • The covenant document arrives → replace the placeholder floor in notebook 07.
  • The client adds new account categories (e.g. supplier invoices) → add them to the GL_DRIVER_MAP table in notebook 05; no code change needed.
  • The roofing-day thresholds need adjusting (a new study, or the foreman disagreeing with the 10 mm cut-off) → edit the THRESHOLDS table in notebook 11.
  • A live weather forecast API comes in → swap the climate-normal lookup in notebook 11 for the API call; the rest of the math is unchanged.

DriverA category of cash movement (sales in, materials out, etc.).
CovenantA rule in a loan contract — typically a minimum cash level.
HeadroomThe slack between current cash and the covenant floor.
Anchor dateThe most recent date in our data. Up to here: real. After: predicted.
Silver / GoldData-engineering stages. Silver = cleaned up. Gold = ready for the dashboard.
Work-day scoreHow productive a single day is for roofing: 1.0 = full, 0.5 = half, 0.0 = lost.
Work-day ratioA weekly number 0–1 telling you what fraction of the work week the weather allowed.
Climate normal"What the weather typically does this month, based on the last 3 years."
